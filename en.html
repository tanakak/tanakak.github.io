Katsuya Tanaka

Professor, Faculty of Economics, Shiga University, Japan

Also: Visiting Researcher, National Research Institute for Earth Science and Disaster Resilience (NIED) · Visiting Professor, Shizuoka University · Global Senior Fellow, Portland State University · Global Fellow, Ewha Womans University

An environmental economist working on how people decide under climate and disaster risk: when warnings lead to action, which adaptation measures residents put first, and how the costs are shared. The main tools are survey experiments (choice experiments, factorial surveys, best–worst scaling) and spatial data analysis, applied with partners in Japan, the United States, Korea and China.

Research

What has to change for societies to adapt to climate risk?

Technology and budgets are not the only things that can change. We look at four conditions — how warnings are communicated, how measures are prioritized and paid for, how cities use natural systems, and how streets are designed — and test how people’s choices and public agreement respond when they change, using resident surveys, experiments and spatial data.

Disaster risk communication

Why do warnings that reach people so often fail to move them?

Key findingIn a survey experiment with 1,324 residents of Hiroshima, evacuation intention turned into action only at about 0.7 on a 0–1 scale, higher than the 0.5 usually assumed. Seeing others evacuate and better privacy in shelters raised the likelihood of evacuating substantially.

Tanaka, Akaishi, Yokota & Kusano (2026), Climate Risk Management · open access · DOI

Climate adaptation

With limited budgets, what do residents put first — and will they pay for it?

Key findingAmong 1,819 Yokohama residents, life-saving heat measures such as stronger emergency medical services clearly ranked above longer-term measures. Yet how strongly people favored the top measure was not associated with support for paying more: priorities and willingness to pay did not line up.

Tanaka & Akaishi (2026), Risk Analysis · DOI

Green infrastructure

Does green infrastructure reduce flood losses, and what makes owners adopt it?

Key findingAcross 226 municipalities in Korea, spatial models show that green infrastructure lowers the combined cost of flood damage and recovery. The effect differs by type and stays within the municipality, with no spillover to neighboring areas.

Han, Kim & Tanaka (2026), Environmental Management · DOI

Streets and urban space

Which street redesigns do residents favor, and how much would they pay?

Key findingAmong 1,717 residents along 19 corridors in Portland, Oregon, median refuge islands and mini roundabouts were the most preferred traffic-calming treatments. Households were willing to pay on average $34 a month for a traffic-calming program, though preferring a treatment did not always mean supporting a program that comes with a cost.

Tanaka, Dill, McNeil, Geller & Kondo (2027), Transportation Research Part A · DOI
